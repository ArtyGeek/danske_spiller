# Native spoken-Danish markers

Evidence that speech is native. Absence of any single item proves nothing; look for the *cluster*. Remember ASR often normalises these away — check how the transcript was produced before reading absence as stiffness.

## Discourse particles (sentence adverbs carrying attitude)
Dense in natural speech, hard for learners to place correctly, and usually dropped by machine-translated or scripted output.

| Particle | Typical function | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **jo** | shared knowledge / "as you know" | "Det er jo ikke min skyld." |
| **altså** | clarification, impatience, restating | "Altså, jeg mener bare…" |
| **da** | obviousness, mild protest | "Det kan du da ikke mene." |
| **vel** | seeking agreement | "Du kommer vel i morgen?" |
| **nok** | probability | "Han er nok gået hjem." |
| **sgu / skam / osse** | emphasis, colloquial | "Det var sgu dyrt." |
| **lige** | softening a request / "just" | "Kan du lige række mig saltet?" |
| **bare / da bare / simpelthen / faktisk / egentlig** | emphasis, contrast | "Jeg ville egentlig bare hjem." |
| **mon / ikke også / ikke?** | wondering; tag questions | "Det var dejligt, ikke?" |

Position matters: these sit after the finite verb in main clauses and **before** it in subordinate clauses ("…fordi det jo er dyrt"). A particle in the wrong slot is a learner signal.

## Fillers, backchannels, interjections
øh, øhm, nå, nåh, ja, jamen, jo, nej, ej, hov, åh, ad, puh, pyt, okay, hva'? Used to hold the floor and react. A long casual passage with *none* is suspicious; a short, task-like passage need not have them.

## Reduction and clipping (spelling shows it only if the transcriber kept it)
- Infinitive marker *at* → [å]; *jeg* → *jæ / æ* (Jutland) or [ja]; *det* → *de*; *hvad* → *hva'*; *ikke* → *ik'*; *skulle/kunne/ville/have* → *sku'/ku'/vil'/ha'*; *nogle/noget* → *nåt*.
- Written Danish forms read aloud in full ("Jeg ved det ikke endnu") are a stiffness signal in casual speech.

## Ellipsis
Natives drop the subject and finite pieces: "Ved ikke.", "Kommer lige.", "Skal du med?", "Nå, går du allerede?" Complete, symmetrical sentences in every turn of a casual dialogue are a scripted-text tell.

## Repairs and false starts
"Jeg skal — altså vi skal ud og —" / "Han er, øh, nej, hun er …". Repairs show real-time planning. They are native evidence, not errors.

## Common native phrasing (collocation level)
Learners and MT tend to pick the literal verb; natives use fixed verbs:
- *give mening*, *træffe en beslutning*, *stille et spørgsmål*, *tage et billede*, *have travlt*, *have det godt*, *være enig*, *glæde sig til*, *kunne godt lide*, *det går fint*, *det var så lidt / selv tak*, *Hvad så?*, *Hvordan går det?*, *Det er (ikke) noget problem*, *jeg har lige …* (just did), *ikke noget at sige til*.
- Time/place: *i weekenden, i morgen tidlig, om aftenen, på arbejde, i skole/på kursus*.
- Perfect with *er* for motion/change of state: *Jeg er kommet*, *Han er blevet syg*.

## Spoken syntax native speakers use
- Fronting with V2: "I går så jeg ham." / "Den bil har jeg aldrig set."
- Cleft/presentational *det er … der/som* and existential *der er*: "Det var Peter, der ringede."
- *man* for generic "you/one": "Man kan jo ikke vide det."
- Left dislocation: "Min bror, han bor i Aarhus."
- Main-clause order after *at/fordi* in speech ("Jeg tror, han kommer ikke") — tolerated, low weight.

## Register layers (all native)
Standard spoken (rigsdansk), regional dialect, social/youth speech, formal speech (talks, news). Judge fit against the *setting*, not against a single norm. Formal pronoun *De* is rare in modern speech; its use in a casual dialogue is a textbook tell.
